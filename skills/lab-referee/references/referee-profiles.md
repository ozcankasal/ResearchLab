# Referee profiles

Choose at least three per paper, so that they disagree for good reasons. The rows below are starting points; take the field insiders and skeptics from the project's own cast.

| Paper type | Profiles |
|---|---|
| Mathematics (proof paper) | the specialist, who checks the key lemma line by line and knows the folklore; the neighbour from an adjacent field, who checks whether the result is new under another name; the skeptic, who asks whether it is a corollary of a known theorem; the reader, who asks whether the paper is readable and the notation consistent |
| Quantum algorithms and complexity | the complexity theorist (input model, lower bounds, proof gaps); the dequantizer (the best classical algorithm with the same access); the experimentalist (resources, noise, data loading); the specialist in the application domain |
| Numerical or simulation study | the statistician (forking paths, error bars, seeds, multiplicity); the domain physicist (physical meaning, limits, units); the research software engineer (reproducibility from the repository); the benchmarking skeptic (baselines, reporting) |
| Physics letter (PRL style) | the specialist, for validity; the broad reader, for importance and interest to physicists outside the subfield; the skeptic, who asks whether the prediction is distinguishable from existing knowledge |
| Empirical study (social, health, life sciences) | the field insider from the cast; the method specialist for the study's mode (causal inference, experimental design, qualitative methods); the statistician; a guardian when people or personal data are involved |
| Review or meta-analysis | the evidence-synthesis specialist; a field insider; the statistician |
| Humanities | the field insider from the cast; the archives specialist; a reader from a neighbouring discipline |
| Methods or software paper | the user, who installs it and runs the example; the maintainer, who looks at tests, licence and documentation; the comparator, who looks at existing tools |

## What every report covers

Following Smith's "The Task of the Referee" and Nature's guidance on referee reports:

1. The recommendation and its main reasons.
2. A summary of the paper in one to five sentences, in the referee's own words.
3. Whether the goal is valid and significant, and whether the result is new. Name the closest prior work, opened and quoted.
4. Correctness, covering method, proofs, statistics and error bars. Name one step or number the referee checked.
5. Clarity and presentation.
6. Required changes, then suggested ones, each with its location.
7. The parts of the paper outside the referee's expertise.

## Common reasons for rejection

The paper is incremental or too specialised for the venue. The introduction gives no context or motivation. References are dated or mostly self-citations. There is no clear punch-line. A physics letter makes a prediction that is not distinguishable from existing knowledge. A mathematical or computational paper has no application to physics, at a physics journal.
